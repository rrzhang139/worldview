# Energy Systems

## Question
Where will the marginal gigawatt of US (and global) power come from in the late 2020s — and which constraint binds first: generation capacity, transmission, gas turbines, nuclear restarts, or interconnection queue?

## Why it matters
Power has gone from "boring utility problem" to "binding constraint on AI buildout, electrification, and reshoring" in 24 months. Hyperscalers are signing nuclear PPAs and behind-the-meter gas deals because the grid cannot accommodate the load on the timeline they need. The energy mix decisions made in the next 5 years (reopening Three Mile Island; new gas plants getting permitted; SMR commercialization) will determine power costs for a generation. This is where Dwarkesh-style energy + AI thinking lives.

## Mental model

**Power is the new bandwidth.**

What bandwidth was to the internet (1995–2010), power is to AI (2024–?). When something is the binding constraint of an industry, three things follow: (a) cost rises sharply at the margin, (b) creative substitutions emerge (behind-the-meter gas turbines, nuclear restarts, geothermal in Wyoming, ammonia-to-power), (c) winners are determined by who can secure the constraint, not who has the best product. **Watch the substitutions to gauge how tight the constraint really is** — a hyperscaler signing a 20-year nuclear PPA tells you more about power scarcity than any macroeconomic statistic. When the substitutions get exotic and expensive, you know the constraint is genuinely binding, not rhetorical.

- ⚠️ 2026-08-13/17: **PJM files its large-load framework at FERC (Docket ER26-3515) and the answer to "who brings the power" becomes explicit: bring your own generation or be curtailed first.** A "Large Load" is ≥50MW cumulative peak at a single electrical site; "New Large Load" means entering service after June 1, 2027. New large loads may energize *before* supporting capacity exists — but any demand not backed by qualifying new capacity is curtailed ahead of Pre-Emergency Load Management Response, under a new Interim Resource Adequacy Service and a mandatory Large Load Registry. PJM also proposes a reliability backstop procurement — an additional capacity auction starting September 2026 — against a ~60GW capacity shortfall. PJM's cited numbers: ~70GW of new large-load demand by 2038, ~15GW of generation retired since 2022, and of 32GW of load growth 2024–2030, **30GW is data centers**. Comments due Sept 3; tariff revisions requested effective Oct 12, 2026. — This resolves the W33 open question, and it resolves it harder than expected: the binding constraint isn't being relieved, it's being *rationed by rule*. Interconnection is no longer first-come-first-served with a queue — it's conditional service where unbacked data-center load is the designated first casualty. ISO-NE is moving the same direction (08-18, Docket EL26-72: expanding a proposal to require new large loads to bring their own new generation); CAISO issued its own large-load proposal 08-14. The FERC Section 206 show-cause responses across all six RTO/ISOs were due Aug 17.
- ⚠️ 2026-08-18: **Pennsylvania Gov. Shapiro signs EO 2026-05, making state data-center standards legally binding** for projects with peak demand >25MW. GRID requirements: power must come from **new generating resources within the same PJM zone**; escalating firm clean power (10% initially → 14.5% after three years → **32% by Jan 1, 2035**); and **full cost causation** — data centers pay "all costs caused in whole or in part by the interconnection, service, or load." Enforcement is via permitting leverage: signers get DEP rolling/parallel permit review, projects previously in the Fast-Track program are stripped out, and no data center is eligible for fast-tracking without binding GRID compliance. The EO also **prohibits NDAs** for data-center projects. — The state-PUC cost-shifting fight flagged in W20 ("watch state PUC decisions on whether data-center load is cross-subsidized by residential ratepayers — that's where the backlash gets teeth") has now produced binding law with a specific clean-power schedule and an explicit cost-causation rule. The NDA prohibition is the underrated part: it removes the confidentiality that has made hyperscaler load deals hard to evaluate at all.
